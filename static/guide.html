What each page does

SPIREXA is eight pages over one pipeline. Three of them are what an operator opens every morning; three are what you show somebody to explain the physics; two are for checking the system's own arithmetic. Each card below says which, what data drives it, and whether any machine learning is involved.

Operational — open these daily

liveML
The dashboard — which slopes to visit today
Scores 311 monitored slopes every morning against today's satellite rainfall and sorts them into four piles: ACT, PREPARE, WATCH, CLEAR. Click any red dot and the impact panel traces what a slide there cuts off — road segments, villages isolated, detour kilometres — on the real OSM road graph. Below the map: where the next sensor should go, and what the GSI records say follows what.

livephysics
Basin monitor — the cascading hazard timeline
One storm marched forward through soil, slope and channel together, so the order of the hazards falls out rather than being assumed. Same 110 mm/day: on dry ground a landslide at +23 h and no flood at all; on ground already 58% wet the river leaves its banks at +10.5 h and the slope goes at +11 h. Combines the landslide score, the GLOF score and the river forecast into one basin risk.

scenariophysics
NHPC Teesta control feed — the 83-minute warning
A control-room view of the four Teesta projects using NHPC's real tag structure (ARMAC, TSI, eAabhas). Toggle between a normal monsoon day and 3–4 October 2023, then between no warning and 90 minutes of notice. Same lake, same breach, same wave: without warning three dams are lost and 5,000 people sit below a breach; with it, none. Scrub the timeline minute by minute.

MLphysics
Glacial lakes — which one, and how long you have
The 276 lakes detected from Sentinel-2 in the 2025 melt season, ranked by what each would do if it burst. Picks out lakes nobody is watching: GL01 holds 173 million m³, four times South Lhonak, and has no name. Peak discharge now comes from a model trained on 502 real outbursts worldwide, reported beside the Froehlich equation the project used to ship alone.

/flood
scenariophysics
Teesta dam cascade — three dams, all adjustable
The real NHPC cascade with real inter-dam distances. Drive it from manual sliders, from the Arduino ground node, or from live satellite. Shows what a dam actually is in a flood: not a pipe but pondage, a spillway rating, a gate that takes half an hour to travel — and, once it fails, its own stored water joining the wave below.

/simulator
scenarioML
3D slope — simulate, live, replay
One hillside, three modes. Simulate is the visitor's sliders. Live pulls a real monitored slope's conditions this morning from IMERG, SMAP and the DEM. Replay takes a recorded failure — the 2008 Itanagar slide that killed 21 — and scores each of the thirty days before it, so the question becomes how much notice there would have been, not whether it would have been flagged.
random forest, live USGS factor of safety lead time is an upper bound

Check the system's own arithmetic

/discharge
ML
River discharge — 7 days ahead, with bands
Twenty-one gradient-boosted models (7 horizons × 3 quantiles) give a p10/p50/p90 fan for the Teesta at Gajoldoba, on top of SCS curve-number runoff rather than raw rain. This is the weakest module in the project and the page says so. It is trained on synthetic hydrology, and it has essentially learned persistence — yesterday's flow matters 45× more than the rain.

/api/…
live
The API — everything above, as JSON
Every page is a thin client over the same endpoints, so anything on screen can be checked directly: /api/risk (today's four piles), /api/impact (what a slide cuts off), /api/glof/model (the learned discharge scorecard), /api/eventgraph/findings (what follows what, counted), /api/scada (the plant feed), /api/replay (a failure, day by day), /api/sync (the offline payload the Android app caches).
What is machine learning here, and what is not. Four trained models: the rainfall trigger (318 dated events, ROC-AUC 0.933), the ground susceptibility model (7,741 field-mapped slides, 0.8622 with road features removed), the GLOF peak-discharge model (502 real outbursts) and the river discharge forecast (synthetic, and weak). Everything else — Huggel, Froehlich, Muskingum, Manning, SCS curve number, the infinite-slope factor of safety — is published physics you can check by hand, and it stays that way on purpose. A trip limit off a protection schedule beats a model you have to trust.
